## A board

Take a real map, look at it from above, and lay graph paper over it. Each square is <N k="game.tile.m" /> on a side and is either wall or floor. A player stands on one square, faces one of eight directions, and every <N k="game.tick.s" /> decides what to do next. A whole game fits in a few kilobytes, so one graphics card plays hundreds of them side by side: <N k="speed.env.sps" alt /> game steps a second on a friend's RTX 2080 Ti.

Plenty is lost. There are no heights, no jumps, no shooting through walls. Sight is tested on squares, which is generous: the grid grants about <N k="sight.grid.extra" alt /> sightlines the real map blocks.

But much of what makes Counter-Strike a game of information survives. There are two bomb sites to attack or hold, and chokepoints between them. There is fog of war: you see an enemy only inside a <N k="sight.fov.deg" />° cone and within <N k="sight.range.m" />, and your team shares a memory of where it last saw each one, like a radar. Running footsteps carry through walls; walking is silent. Every player carries a smoke, a molotov, a flash and an HE grenade, and money and weapons carry over from round to round.

Sight is where the board gets interesting. To decide whether you can see me, the game samples points along the line between us; if any of them falls in a wall or in a smoke, the line is blocked. Figure 2 puts five defenders and five attackers on Mirage in the middle of a round and draws every pair of enemies that can see each other. One twist is worth testing: a player standing just inside the edge of a smoke can still be seen from outside it, but cannot see anyone.

## Learning

Now put an agent on the board. It is one neural network, copied into every player, and each copy sees only what its own player sees: the walls nearby, its teammates, what its team remembers about the enemy, the sounds it heard, its money. All of that arrives as <N k="obs.width" /> numbers, turned so that forward is always up.

Every tick the network answers a few questions in a fixed order. First, where to step. Then, run or walk. Then, knowing those two answers, which way to turn, what to do with its hands (shoot, or throw one of its four grenades) and at what: an enemy, or a spot on the map. Asking one question at a time, instead of all at once, was the first change that made learning take off.

Learning itself is trial, error and bookkeeping. The agent plays <N k="train.batch.games" /> games at once, <N k="train.batch.steps" /> decisions each, then looks back: which decisions came before rounds its team won? Proximal policy optimisation, PPO, nudges those decisions to be a little more likely, and never by much in one step. The reward is the plainest one I could think of: win the round, plus one; lose it, minus one. Nothing in between.

<Note>

**Two formulas.** PPO weighs each decision by its advantage *A*, how much better it went than expected, and by the ratio *r* of its new to its old probability, clipped so one update cannot move it far:

$$
L = \mathbb{E}\,\min\big(rA,\ \hat r A\big)
$$

$$
\hat r = \operatorname{clip}(r,\ 1-\epsilon,\ 1+\epsilon)
$$

Here ε is <N k="ppo.clip" />. Ratings come from Bradley and Terry's model: the chance of winning a round depends only on the rating gap Δ.

$$
P(\text{win}) = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{-\Delta/400}}
$$

</Note>

Who does it play against? Half the time, itself. Half the time, a pool of its own past versions, so it cannot forget how to handle an old trick. And in <N k="train.bots.share" alt />, scripted bots with simple plans, rush B and the like, so it never loses the knack of punishing them. Figure 4 shows the whole machine, from map generator to tournament, and the hardware under it.

Some things I expected to matter did not. A memory of the round so far improved the agent's guesses about hidden enemies by about <N k="memory.gru.gain" />. Residual layers, which help deep networks elsewhere, did nothing here. What helped was a smaller learning rate.

How do I know the agent improved at all? I let it play its past selves and count wins, and I report the result in Elo. The one thing to know about Elo is that it measures odds, not points. Drag the first number in this sentence: <EloSentenceBlock />.

## Practice maps

The biggest surprise of the project came in its first week. My best agents had learned on open, randomly generated arenas, and they were good there. Then I gave them the five real maps, and they fell apart: their attack win rate against simple scripted bots dropped to between <N k="maps.zeroshot.attack.low" /> and <N k="maps.zeroshot.attack.high" />.

Half of their moves were failing, and the reason was one rule. Moves are relative to where a player faces, so an agent facing diagonally in a corridor one square wide could not step anywhere: every move ran into a wall. Real maps are full of such corridors. Letting a blocked step slide along the wall fixed it.

Then the real question: should agents practise on the maps they will be tested on? I tried both. Against training on four real maps, training only on <N k="maps.generator.count" /> generated ones came out about <N k="maps.genvsreal.mirage" /> Elo ahead on Mirage, a real map neither had seen. The agents trained on real maps had memorised them. Since then my agents never train on a real map. All five are exams.

So the generator matters. A generated map is a recipe: two bomb sites, a spawn for each team, long and short routes between them, a middle, chokepoints. Figure 5 rolls maps from three versions of it. The third adds narrow doors with an elbow, a site on each side of the map and two ways into each.

## Measured honestly

"Stronger" needs a referee, and mine is a tournament. Every pair of agents plays <N k="eval.rounds.progress" /> rounds in each direction, attacking and defending, on all five real maps. Every recipe is trained twice, from two independent seeds, and each run is compared only with its own ancestor.

Why so fast? The answer is a quirk of time, and my favourite thing in the project. The game moves in ticks of <N k="game.tick.s" />: everyone decides, then everything happens at once. To shoot at an enemy, a defender must have seen it at the end of a tick, and the shot lands on the next tick, after everyone has moved. So an attacker who steps into view and straight back is never there when the bullet arrives. The shot hits the empty square it just left. Figure 10 lets you step through it, one tick at a time.

<FreePeekBlock number={10}>
  <span slot="caption">A one-tick peek is hit <N k="peek.hit.nosnap" /> of the time; with snap fire, <N k="peek.hit.snap" />.</span>
  <span slot="try">Step out, step back, then turn on snap fire.</span>
</FreePeekBlock>

Peeking is free, and the agents found it. When an attacker walks into the sight of a defender who is already aiming, the exposure lasts a single tick <N k="peek.onetick.share" /> of the time. In <N k="peek.stepback.agent" /> of the single-tick looks it survives, the attacker steps straight back to where it came from; scripted bots do that <N k="peek.stepback.bots" alt /> of the time. The defender hits only <N k="peek.hit.nosnap" /> of these one-tick peeks — <Stamp measuredOn={on("peek.onetick.share")} /> — so stepping out to look costs almost nothing.

Defenders learned the same lesson from the other side. At the moment of first contact, <N k="holds.ct.moving.contact" /> of the agents' defenders are moving; scripted bots, <N k="holds.bots.ct.moving.contact" />. Fifteen seconds into a round, <N k="holds.ct.moving.15s" /> of agent defenders are still on the move. Among pros at that moment, only <N k="holds.pros.ct.moving.15s" /> are.

Is it just the grid's generous sight? No. With sight computed on the real map geometry instead of on squares, first contact moves only to <N k="tempo.mesh.contact.median" />. Scripted bots on the very same grid wait until <N k="tempo.bots.contact.median" />. The rules invite the rush, and the agents accept.

So I changed the rules. With snap fire, a defender who is set and aiming fires at whoever steps into its aim on that same tick. The same one-tick peek is now hit <N k="peek.hit.snap" /> of the time, and scripted bots with the rule win <N k="snap.bots.holder" /> of their duels as the player holding the angle, inside the pros' range: <N k="pros.holder" ci />. The agents trained with snap fire step back less often. They still make first contact just as early.

Learning found the cheapest strategy the rules allow. That is not a bug in the learner; it is the most honest thing it could tell me about my game. If I want agents that play the way Counter-Strike is played, the game has to make Counter-Strike's choices the cheap ones.

The molotov taught me patience. Early molotovs flew wherever the agent happened to face and mostly burned the thrower, so the agents learned never to throw one. A two-stage lesson fixed that: aimed throws with no damage to the thrower first, then the real rules back on. Given a safe place to explore, the agents found the tool; given the real rules again, they kept what worked.

The same trick failed for flashes. Agents learned to throw them and kept blinding themselves; nudged toward the bots' habits, they threw more and still blinded themselves. Flashes are parked.

Once I trained a second learner beside the main one, with a single job: find holes. It never found one worth having. It sat at <N k="exploiter.winrate.low" /> to <N k="exploiter.winrate.high" /> against the main line. All <N k="exploiter.lives" /> of its lives ended on a timer, not on a breakthrough, and dropping it made training faster at no cost.

The agents never save money, and for a while I blamed the economy. It was the discount. A learner values a reward less the further away it is. I had set the next round to count <N k="ppo.gamma.round" /> as much as this one, but each round's own win arrives dozens of ticks into it, so in practice the next round counted about <N k="econ.nextround.weight" />. A saved rifle is worth roughly as much in my game as in pro matches; the agent just could not see that far. Four attempts later, saving still has not stuck.

And the one that stung: a fresh start from scratch stood at <N k="elo.restart.scratch" /> Elo against the line it was meant to replace when I stopped it.

## Toward real CS2

What would close the gap? Looking back, the square was doing four jobs at once: it was the player's body, the unit of every wall, the eye that tests sight, and the length of one step. Real Counter-Strike keeps those apart, and the next version of the game will too. The plan is one simulation core in CS2's own units with three clocks: decisions every <N k="game.tick.s" /> as today, fights and perception on a much faster clock, and movement at the server's <N k="cs2.tickrate" /> updates a second. That is how large game-playing agents already work: OpenAI Five, AlphaStar and GT Sophy all decide far less often than their games update. The square game stays as a baseline, and today's agents move over once, by distillation.

Some first evidence is in. On maps compiled from CS2's own navigation data, the agent after block 28 keeps <N k="elo.head.compiled" /> of its <N k="elo.head.block2" /> Elo. On real CS floors, with their ramps and drops, today's agent's defence against scripted bots falls from <N k="zeroshot.floors.defend.before" /> to <N k="zeroshot.floors.defend.after" /> without any retraining. A real CS2 dedicated server now runs at <N k="referee.speedup" />× speed as a referee, about <N k="referee.rounds.perhour" /> bot rounds an hour, so agents can one day be checked inside the real game instead of my copy of it. And from <N k="data.pros.maps" /> pro maps I have extracted what the players actually did on every decision step: <N k="data.pros.labels" /> labelled decisions to learn from later.

None of this is the goal yet. No agent has played a human or a real CS2 match yet. The goal has not changed: agents that can hold their own against a professional team. Until then, this page is a measurement of the distance.

## Methods

**Game.** A 28×28 grid of squares <N k="game.tile.m" /> wide; one decision every <N k="game.tick.s" />; rounds of up to <N k="game.round.s" /> (CS2: <N k="cs2.round.s" />). Planting takes <N k="game.plant.s" />, defusing <N k="game.defuse.s" />, and the bomb goes off after <N k="game.bomb.s" />. Sight: a <N k="sight.fov.deg" />° cone out to <N k="sight.range.m" />, blocked by walls and smoke. One smoke, molotov, flash and HE grenade per player per round; money, weapons and armour across the halves of a match.

**Agent.** <N k="obs.width" /> observation numbers per player, turned to the player's facing. Five action heads, sampled in order: move (<N k="act.head.move" />), walk (<N k="act.head.walk" />), then turn (<N k="act.head.turn" />), hands (<N k="act.head.fire" />) and target (<N k="act.head.target" />). Two hidden layers of <N k="net.hidden.width" /> with LayerNorm, <N k="net.params" alt /> parameters, and a separate critic. Every player learns from its own view, with weights shared across players.

**Learning.** PPO with clip <N k="ppo.clip" />, GAE λ <N k="ppo.gae" />, discount <N k="ppo.gamma" /> per tick within a round and <N k="ppo.gamma.round" /> between rounds; <N k="train.batch.games" /> games × <N k="train.batch.steps" /> steps per update. A training block is <N k="train.block.envsteps" alt /> game steps; the gain over block 2 took <N k="train.blocks.gain" /> blocks, <N k="train.envsteps.total" alt /> steps in all.

**Evaluation.** Round-robin tournaments on Dust2, Mirage, Inferno, Ancient and Anubis, both sides, sampled actions: <N k="eval.rounds.progress" /> rounds per ordered pair for progress, <N k="eval.rounds.ab" /> for A/B tests. Bradley–Terry ratings on the Elo scale with bootstrap intervals; two independent seeds, each against its own ancestor. These are self-play ratings only: no human or real CS2 opponent has been rated.

**Pros.** <N k="data.pros.maps" /> pro maps of 2026 (<N k="data.pros.tier1" /> tier-one LAN, <N k="data.pros.tier2" /> tier-two), <N k="data.pros.rounds" /> rounds, with intervals from resampling whole matches. Agent first contact is the first tick any agent sees an enemy. Pro first contact is the earliest of the first "spotted" flag, the first bullet aimed near a visible enemy and the first damage, counted from the end of freeze time. It can only err late, which is why I write "about".
